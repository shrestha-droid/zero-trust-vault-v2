Shamir secret sharing, explained.

Shamir secret sharing splits a secret into N shares so that any K of them rebuild it exactly, and any fewer than K reveal nothing at all. Adi Shamir (the S in RSA) published it in 1979. It's how you give a secret to a group without trusting any one member of it.

The idea in one picture

Two points define a straight line; one point doesn't. If your secret is where a line crosses the vertical axis, you can hand out points on that line. Anyone with two points draws the line and reads the secret. Anyone with one point knows nothing: infinitely many lines pass through it, crossing the axis at every possible value.

Generalise: K points define a polynomial of degree K−1. Pick a random polynomial whose constant term is your secret, give each person one point on it, and any K of them can rebuild it by interpolation.

Why fewer shares reveal nothing

This is the part that makes it special. It isn't "hard to break" the way encryption is; it's information-theoretically secure. With K−1 shares, every possible secret is exactly equally likely. No amount of computing power, quantum or otherwise, changes that. Real implementations do the arithmetic over a finite field (usually GF(2⁸), one byte at a time) so that this holds exactly.

Where it's used

  • Key ceremonies: root keys for certificate authorities and DNSSEC are split between keyholders.
  • Crypto wallets: SLIP-39 seed phrases and many custody products split the seed this way.
  • Break-glass access: production root credentials that need two of three directors.
  • Inheritance: letting family recover a secret together, but not individually. See crypto inheritance.

Pitfalls people hit

  • Rolling your own. Field arithmetic, randomness and side channels are easy to get subtly wrong. Use an audited library.
  • No integrity check. Plain Shamir can't tell a correct share from a typo or a forgery; it just rebuilds the wrong secret. You need checksums per share and a way to detect a bad one.
  • Splitting the secret directly. Each share is as long as the secret, so splitting a 100 MB file gives everyone 100 MB. The usual fix: encrypt the data with a random key and split only the key.
  • Rebuilding on an online machine. At the moment of recovery the whole secret exists in one place. Do it somewhere you trust, ideally offline.
  • Never testing recovery. A share that was mistyped on day one is only discovered on the worst day.

A free tool that handles the pitfalls

Zero-Trust Vault uses Shamir the recommended way: it encrypts your secret or file with AES-256-GCM, then splits only the key, using the shamir-secret-sharing library (audited by Cure53 and Zellic) unmodified. Each share has its own checksum, the key has a fingerprint, and if you supply more than K shares it finds a consistent set and names any forged ones. Everything runs in your browser, in an isolated worker; nothing is uploaded. Thresholds from 2 to 16 shares, printable recovery kits with QR codes, and a Verify tab to test recovery without decrypting.
