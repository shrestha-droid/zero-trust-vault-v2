How to back up a seed phrase safely.

A seed phrase has two ways to fail: you lose it, or someone else finds it. Most backup advice fixes one by making the other worse. Here is every common option, compared honestly, including the one that fixes both.

The two rules

  • Never type it into anything online in plain text. Not a notes app, not an email to yourself, not a photo, not a cloud document. Those get synced, backed up, indexed and breached.
  • Never keep exactly one copy. One copy is one fire, one flood or one house move away from gone.

The trouble is that "more copies" breaks the first rule's spirit: every extra plain copy is another place it can be found.

Why "split it in half" is a trap

It feels like two people each holding half is safe. It isn't. Each word in a standard phrase carries 11 bits, so someone holding six of twelve words has only about 64 bits left to guess, a fraction of the original 128. Worse, it has no redundancy: if either half is lost, so is the wallet. Proper splitting (Shamir secret sharing) gives each person a share that reveals nothing on its own, and lets you lose some shares safely.

The setup that survives both failures

  1. Keep your steel or paper backup in one secure place, as the copy you'd use day to day.
  2. Make an encrypted copy of the seed phrase (plus the passphrase, if you use one) on an offline device.
  3. Split the key 2 of 3 or 3 of 5. Store the shares in different places: a home safe, a bank deposit box, a family member's house, your lawyer.
  4. Store the encrypted vault anywhere, even in the cloud. Without enough shares it's random noise.
  5. Test recovery from the shares before you rely on them.

Now a burglar who finds one share gets nothing, a fire that takes one location costs you nothing, and if something happens to you, your family can still get in. That last point is the start of a crypto inheritance plan.

Doing it with Zero-Trust Vault

Zero-Trust Vault encrypts the phrase on your device with AES-256-GCM, splits the key with an independently audited Shamir library, and prints a recovery-kit page with a QR code for each share. Each share carries a checksum, so a typo is caught on entry, and given one more share than the threshold, a forged share is named rather than silently accepted. You can download the app as a single HTML file and use it on an offline computer, with an air-gap mode that makes the browser itself refuse network access. It's free and open source.

Hardware wallets with Shamir built in

Some hardware wallets support SLIP-39, a standard that splits the wallet seed itself into share phrases. If yours does and you're setting up a new wallet, it's a good option for that wallet. An encrypted vault is still useful for everything around it: the PIN, the passphrase, exchange 2FA codes, and instructions for your family.
