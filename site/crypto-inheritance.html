Crypto inheritance: how to pass on crypto when you die.

Self-custody means nobody can take your coins. It also means nobody can recover them for your family. Billions in crypto are already lost for good because the only person who knew the seed phrase is gone. Here is how to avoid that, without handing anyone the keys early.

The problem in one sentence

Whoever can read your seed phrase owns your crypto, so you need your family to be able to read it after you're gone and nobody to be able to read it before. Every inheritance plan is a trade-off between those two.

Why "put it in the will" doesn't work

  • Wills become public. In many countries a will is read by lawyers, courts and executors, and after probate it can become a public record. A seed phrase in it is a seed phrase given away.
  • Your lawyer becomes a target. Anyone who holds the full phrase can move the funds today, and nobody would notice until it's too late.
  • It goes stale. Change wallets once and the will points at an empty one.

The will should say that the crypto exists and who should get it. It should not contain the phrase.

The four ways people actually do it

How it worksThe catch
Tell one personA spouse or child knows the phrase, or where it's written down.They can take it early, lose it, or be pressured into revealing it. One point of failure.
Custodial exchangeLeave the coins on an exchange; heirs claim them with a death certificate.It's not self-custody: the exchange can freeze, fail or be hacked. Claims take months.
Multisig walletThe wallet needs, say, 2 of 3 keys to spend. Heirs hold some keys.Strong, but heirs have to run multisig software correctly under stress, and it only covers that one wallet.
Split the secretEncrypt the phrase, then split the key so that any K of N people can rebuild it.Needs the people to cooperate and to keep their piece. Fewer than K learn nothing.

Splitting is the one that works for any secret, not just one wallet: the seed phrase, the hardware wallet PIN, the exchange 2FA backup codes and a note saying which coins are where.

How splitting works

It's called Shamir secret sharing, and it's maths rather than trust. You pick a threshold, for example 3 of 5. The key is turned into five shares. Any three rebuild it exactly; two, or one, reveal nothing at all about it, not even a single character. So your brother can't open it alone, a burglar who finds one share gets nothing, and your family can still get in if two of the five shares are lost.

A plan you can set up in an afternoon

  1. Write the instructions. Which wallets, which exchanges, which coins, and what you want to happen. Plain language, as if to someone who has never used crypto.
  2. Seal the seed phrase and the instructions together in an encrypted vault, and split its key 3 of 5 (or 2 of 3 for a smaller family).
  3. Give one share to each person, printed as a recovery kit page, and tell them what it is. A spouse, adult children, a sibling, a close friend, your lawyer. Mix people who don't all live in one house.
  4. Keep the encrypted vault in two places nobody can lose at once: your own cloud drive, and a USB stick with the shares. The vault file alone is useless without enough shares.
  5. Run a recovery drill. Check that the shares actually rebuild the key before you need them to. Then repeat once a year, or whenever you change wallets.
  6. Mention it in your will: "My digital assets are in an encrypted vault. My executor will find the instructions in the recovery kit held by…". No phrase, no keys.

Common questions

What if one of my trustees dies or loses their share?

That's what the threshold is for. With 3 of 5, any two shares can be lost. If you lose one, re-key the vault and hand out new shares; the old ones then stop working.

Can my trustees team up and take it early?

If enough of them agree, yes, and no scheme can prevent that. Choose people who wouldn't, and set the threshold so that it takes more than one household to reach it.

Is this a legal will?

No. It's a technical tool that makes the keys recoverable. Who is legally entitled to the assets is a question for your will and a lawyer where you live.
